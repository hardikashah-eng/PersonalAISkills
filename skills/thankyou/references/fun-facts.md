# Fun facts: a light P.S. at the end

Shared by ThankYou, Reconnect and Grateful. When it fits, end the note with one
short, true, delightful fact related to the topic (teamwork, friendship, family,
mentors, gratitude, the occasion, or the shared moment), written as a P.S.:

> P.S. Fun fact: the word "mentor" comes from Mentor, the friend Odysseus trusted
> to guide his son in Homer's *Odyssey*. You were mine.

## Rules

1. **True, with a source.** Use a fact from the bank below. If none fits, find
   one with web search, confirm it in an original or authoritative source (the
   study, a university or government page, a major dictionary or encyclopedia),
   and record the source in `note.json` (`fun_fact.source`). Never use a fact
   from memory alone, and drop it if you're unsure.
2. **Related and light.** It should connect to the relationship, the moment or
   the occasion, and make the reader smile. One or two sentences, ideally under
   35 words. A short warm tie-back to the note is welcome ("You were mine.").
3. **Kind and safe.** No facts about illness, death, disasters, war or
   politics, no grim origins (skip any tradition whose history is painful), no
   stereotypes about cultures, genders or ages, nothing that could read as a
   lecture or as advice ("studies show you should…"), and nothing about the
   recipient's private life.
4. **When applicable, not always.** Skip it for condolence or hard-time notes,
   very short chat messages, formal notes where it would feel flippant, or
   when no fact truly fits. Reconnect messages use one only if it's playful
   and tied to the shared memory.
5. **In the note's language.** Translate the fact naturally; keep names and
   quoted words in their original form.
6. **No numbers on the card's entries.** Entries in a list use hand-drawn
   marks, not 1, 2, 3; the renderer handles this. In plain text they use ✦.

## Fact bank (verified)

### Gratitude and thank-you notes
- In a 2018 study, people who wrote thank-you letters underestimated how
  surprised and happy their recipients would feel, and overestimated how
  awkward the recipients would find it. Source: Kumar & Epley, "Undervaluing
  Gratitude", *Psychological Science* (2018); University of Chicago Booth
  press release.
- Benjamin Franklin described "paying it forward" in a 1784 letter: he lent
  money and asked that, instead of repaying him, the borrower lend it to the
  next honest person in need. Source: Franklin to Benjamin Webb, 22 April 1784,
  Founders Online (US National Archives).

### Reaching out and friendship
- Across 13 experiments, people consistently underestimated how much others
  appreciate an unexpected check-in, and the more surprising the hello, the
  more it was appreciated. Source: Liu, Rim, Min & Min, "The Surprise of
  Reaching Out", *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* (2022);
  American Psychological Association press release.
- It takes roughly 50 hours together to go from acquaintance to casual friend,
  about 90 to become friends, and more than 200 to become close friends.
  Source: Jeffrey Hall, *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships* (2018);
  University of Kansas.
- The word "companion" comes from Latin *com* ("with") + *panis* ("bread"): someone
  you share bread with. Source: standard etymological dictionaries.

### Teams
- Northern bald ibises flying in a V take turns at the front, the most tiring
  spot, and pairs closely match the time each spends leading and following.
  Source: Voelkl et al., *PNAS* (2015); University of Oxford.

### Family
- Killer whale grandmothers that are no longer raising calves of their own
  improve the survival of their grandcalves. Source: Nattrass et al., *PNAS*
  (2019); Universities of York and Exeter.

### Mentors and teachers
- "Mentor" comes from Mentor, the friend Odysseus asked to watch over his son
  Telemachus in Homer's *Odyssey*. Source: standard dictionaries.

### Occasions
- Thanksgiving became a national US holiday in 1863, after the editor Sarah
  Josepha Hale spent 17 years writing to officials, including five presidents,
  asking for it. Source: National Park Service, Library of Congress (Hale's
  letter to Lincoln, 28 September 1863).
- "Diwali" comes from the Sanskrit *dīpāvali*, "a row of lights". Source:
  standard dictionaries.

### Everyday things
- "Chai" and "tea" both come from Chinese words for the drink: the *chá*-like
  words travelled mostly by land, and *te* from the Min dialect travelled
  mostly by sea. Source: World Atlas of Language Structures, "Tea" (Dahl).

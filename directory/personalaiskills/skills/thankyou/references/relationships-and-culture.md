# Relationship, triggers, and culture

The same moment is thanked differently by a spouse, a younger sibling, and a
junior colleague. Before writing, settle three things: **who they are to the
sender**, **what could hurt them**, and **what their culture expects**.

## 1. Relationship → tone

Identify the relationship from what the user says first; from connectors only as
supporting evidence (how they address each other, sign-offs, emoji use). If it is
unclear and it changes the tone, ask once.

| Relationship | Tone | Address / sign-off | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spouse / partner | intimate, tender, playful if that's their way | pet name only if the user uses it; "Love," / "Always," | keep it tasteful — cards get seen by kids and guests; no private details |
| Parent | reverent, warm, grateful for the long arc | "Mom," "Papa," "Amma," — as the user calls them; "Your son/daughter," | don't lecture or sound like a speech; one specific memory beats a life summary |
| Grandparent / elder relative | respectful, gentle, simple language | honorifics ("Dadi," "Nana," "Grandma"); formal "you" where the language has one | large-print friendly, short sentences; avoid slang and English idioms in a translation |
| Child (adult or young) | proud, encouraging, never patronising | first name / nickname; "Love, Dad" | for kids: age-appropriate words, no pressure ("make us proud") |
| Sibling | affectionate, can be lightly playful | nickname if used | gentle shared jokes only — never teasing about weight, looks, career, relationships |
| Close friend | warm, casual, personal | first name / nickname | humour is fine if it's clearly loving; no inside jokes that embarrass if seen |
| In-laws | warm but slightly formal | as the user addresses them | avoid anything that could be read as comparing families |
| Mentor / teacher | respectful, humble, specific about growth | "Dear Prof. Rao," "Sir/Ma'am," "-sensei" per their norm | don't over-familiarise; credit them without flattery |
| Manager / senior colleague | warm-professional | first name if that's the workplace norm | avoid anything that reads as currying favour; no gifts/money mentions |
| Peer / teammate | warm, collegial | first name | keep it about the work + the person; nothing for HR to blink at |
| Direct report / junior | appreciative, specific, empowering | first name | avoid sounding like a performance review; never condescending |
| Neighbour / community / service person | friendly, sincere | "Dear Mr. Lee," or first name as the user uses | keep it simple; no assumptions about their life |
| Ex-partner / estranged family | only if the user insists; neutral, brief, no reopening of the past | — | gently check with the user first; no romance, no blame, no "we should…" |

A note to a person who is senior *and* family (e.g. father-in-law who is also a
mentor) uses the more respectful of the two tones.

## 2. Triggers — topics that could hurt the recipient

Before writing, ask the user (once, briefly) if anything is tender for the
recipient right now, *unless* the context already makes it clear:
"Anything I should steer clear of for them — a recent loss, a hard year?"

Also watch for signals in what the user shares or the connectors show, and
**silently steer around** them — never name the trigger in the note:

- Recent bereavement or anniversary of a loss → no "family gathered round the table" imagery; acknowledge gently only if the user wants ("thinking of you this season").
- Divorce / separation / breakup → no couple or "you two" references.
- Infertility, pregnancy loss, adoption journeys → no references to children, babies, "family growing".
- Job loss, layoffs, business failure → no career-success framing ("on to bigger things!"); focus on who they are.
- Illness (theirs or a loved one's) → no health wishes beyond a gentle "wishing you peace and rest"; no "stay strong".
- Estrangement from family → no assumptions about holiday family plans.
- Body image / eating → no food-centric jokes or comments about appearance.
- Financial strain → no gift talk, travel, or "treat yourself".
- Holidays they don't celebrate → neutral "this season" / occasion-free wording.
- Loneliness or living alone → avoid "enjoy your big family dinner".

If a trigger touches the moment itself (e.g. they helped during a loss), the
note may acknowledge it only in the gentlest abstract form the user approves
("you were there in the hardest weeks"), never with details.

Never store or repeat what you learn about triggers beyond this note.

## 3. Cultural norms

Apply when the user mentions the recipient's culture, or when the shared
language/occasion makes it relevant. Don't infer culture from a name or photo;
when it matters and is unclear, ask.

**Address and formality**
- Many cultures expect titles/honorifics for elders, teachers, and seniors:
  "ji" / "Sir" / "Madam" (India), "-san" / "-sensei" (Japan), "Herr/Frau" +
  surname with *Sie* (Germany, formal), *usted* (many Spanish-speaking contexts),
  *vous* (French formal), 您 (Chinese formal). First names for elders can read as rude.
- Some cultures place family name first (China, Japan, Korea, Hungary) — use the
  form the user uses.

**Holidays and religion**
- Don't assume Thanksgiving or Christmas. Use the occasion the user named; otherwise neutral.
- Match greetings to the holiday: "Happy Diwali" / "Shubh Deepavali", "Eid Mubarak",
  "Chag Sameach", "新年快乐", "Feliz Navidad" — only for people who celebrate it.
- Avoid religious blessings unless the user wants them; when they do, use the
  recipient's tradition, not the sender's.

**Expressing gratitude**
- Some cultures prefer understated thanks between close family (effusive thanks
  can feel distancing — e.g. in many South Asian and East Asian families). Keep
  it warm but less formal; a specific memory says more than superlatives.
- In many East Asian contexts, modesty matters: praise the effort and kindness,
  avoid singling the person out as superior to others.
- In hierarchical workplaces, a junior thanking a senior should be respectful and
  humble; a senior thanking a junior should be generous and specific.

**Imagery and colour (for the art)**
- White and chrysanthemums are linked to mourning in parts of East Asia and
  Europe; avoid for celebratory notes to those recipients.
- The number 4 is unlucky in Chinese/Japanese/Korean contexts — avoid four of a
  motif (use 3, 5, or 8). 8 and red are auspicious in Chinese contexts.
- Avoid alcohol imagery for recipients who may not drink (e.g. observant Muslims)
  and pork/beef food imagery where relevant; harvest grains, flowers, and
  lanterns are safe.
- Left-hand / feet / owl / specific animal symbolism varies — when unsure, use
  nature motifs (sun, leaves, mountains, water, stars).

**Gender and family roles**
- Don't assume roles ("tell your wife…"). Use names or neutral wording.
- Respect the recipient's own forms of address for themselves and their family.

When a cultural rule conflicts with what the user explicitly asks for, follow the
user, but mention the concern in one line if it might cause offence.
